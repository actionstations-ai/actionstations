# Prairie Gold Turkey Cooperative — Biosecurity and Business Continuity Plan

Prairie Gold Turkey Cooperative, Larkin, Minnesota · Plan PG-BIO-2026 · Revised July 2026

## 1. The cooperative

Prairie Gold Turkey Cooperative is a fictional farmer-owned cooperative in Calder County, Minnesota, with 31 member families and 36 contract growing premises across Calder, Pell and Ritter counties (22 of them inside Calder County). It operates a hatchery, a feed mill in Larkin and a processing plant in Dunmore that handles about 38,000 birds a week and employs 410 people. Prairie Gold is a fictional business; real agencies appear only as outside parties.

Biosecurity manager: Gretchen Oyelaran (555-0171). Complex managers: Brett Sandoval (Larkin Ridge Turkey Complex, four barns, 62,000 tom turkeys), Lena Halvorsen (Prairie Wind complex, three barns, 45,000 hens). Company veterinarian: Dr. Anders Whitfield (555-0173). Dispatch and hauling manager: Ruth Calloway (555-0176). Communications: Ivan Petrescu (555-0179).

## 2. Routine biosecurity

- Every farm has a line of separation (a marked boundary), a single entry, a log of people and vehicles, boot and coverall change, and a clean and dirty side. Non-essential visitors are not allowed in the barns.
- Wild bird contact: barns are screened, feed storage is closed, water sources are inspected after fall migration begins; ponds within 150 meters of a barn are flagged and discouraged.
- Weekly mortality is recorded; a mortality rate above 0.5 percent in a day or a drop in water consumption of more than 20 percent triggers a call to the company veterinarian within 2 hours.
- Dead birds are composted on farm under state rules. Feed trucks, shavings trucks and catch crews arrive clean, and catch crews are not permitted to visit more than one premises in 72 hours without cleaning and disinfection.

## 3. Detection and reporting

When mortality exceeds the trigger, the complex manager calls Dr. Whitfield. If HPAI is suspected, Dr. Whitfield calls the Minnesota Board of Animal Health, which sends a state or federal veterinarian to sample, and ships samples to the Minnesota Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory. The complex manager stops all movements from the barn, stops the crews, and tells Gretchen Oyelaran, who tells the cooperative's general manager, Tobias Leclerc (555-0170). The cooperative does not announce anything to the press before the Board does.

## 4. If a Prairie Gold premises is a presumptive positive

1. Hold order and quarantine accepted at once; all trucks leaving the premises stopped; the crews already on site stay and are logged.
2. Gretchen Oyelaran provides the Board and APHIS with the premises' trace data: truck and crew movements for the previous 14 days, feed deliveries, shavings, litter and dead haul, and every farm the same crews visited. The dispatch system, Ruth Calloway's team, produces the list within 4 hours.
3. A contract-grower communications call is held within 6 hours: what is known, what each grower must do, who to call. Growers without a case are told which of their movements will be allowed by permit.
4. The company gives the Board access for depopulation and designates a cleaning and disinfection contractor.
5. The plant in Dunmore decides, with the Board, whether to continue processing birds from unaffected premises. The plant must hold birds in barns, which means welfare and space limits become a problem after about 10 days for tom turkeys approaching 20 weeks.

## 5. Continuity of business

The cooperative's risks if a control area is set over its growing region:

- **Birds ready for market.** 11 premises have flocks at 17 to 20 weeks and need to be moved within 14 days. Moving them requires permits, a negative test and a route.
- **Poults.** The hatchery delivers about 52,000 poults a week; a placement on a premises inside the infected zone is canceled, and poults for other farms are rerouted, with the Board's permission.
- **Feed.** The Larkin feed mill is inside the 10 km radius of the Larkin Ridge Turkey Complex; it needs a permit to deliver and a washing station at the exit. Feed inventory at farms: 4 to 6 days.
- **Dead haul.** Rendering pickups need a permit; composting on farm is the first option.
- **People.** About 140 catch and barn workers; some are immigrants with limited English. Worker health, antiviral prophylaxis and an interpreter are needed.
- **Contracts and trade.** Export certificates are held by the Board; the cooperative informs its buyers, a regional grocery distributor and two export brokers, only after the Board's public notice.

## 6. Decisions held by the cooperative

- Which premises to propose to the Board for first depopulation when crews are limited (usually the ones with the largest number of birds and the closest to other premises).
- Whether to reduce the weekly kill at the plant, and what to tell the 410 plant employees.
- When to release the grower list to the Board and the county EOC.
- What the cooperative's own press statement says; the cooperative uses the state's counts.

## 7. Messages

The cooperative's statement is approved by Tobias Leclerc and uses Board numbers. Content: what happened at one premises (a fictional name or a code), what the cooperative has done, that no food safety risk exists to consumers from properly cooked poultry, that other farms continue to operate under state permits, and where growers and employees call (cooperative line 555-0168).

## 8. Employee health

Anyone who enters an infected barn reports to Calder County Public Health for monitoring and an offer of antivirals. Workers with illness in the 10 days after exposure stay home and call the county line. The cooperative pays for the time off.

## 9. Recovery

After depopulation, cleaning and disinfection (a multi-step process inspected by APHIS), environmental testing and a fallow period, the Board allows restocking. The cooperative estimates 8 to 12 weeks from the day of depopulation to restocking.
