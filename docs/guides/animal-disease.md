# Animal disease response

The animal disease toolkit gives an exercise the work of the people who respond to a foreign animal disease: premises and the zones around them, movement permits, sampling, depopulation and disposal, and the notices that go to producers, fair exhibitors and consumers. It suits state animal health offices, poultry integrators, dairy cooperatives, extension and fair boards, veterinary school classes, and the county emergency managers and health departments that support them.

Turn it on in the scenario's **Toolkits** tab (see [Toolkits](toolkits.md)). The app proposes it when the brief mentions avian influenza, a control area, a state veterinarian, livestock or a county fair, and for the food and agriculture, public health, municipal, campus and emergency communications kinds of organization. The **Animal health, veterinary and agriculture** kind of organization describes the response teams themselves and suggests the epidemiology, weather and flooding toolkits too.

The toolkit stays at coordination level. It carries no detail on handling or changing any pathogen, and every farm, owner and person it produces is fictional. Follow your own state plan, which governs, and have a state veterinarian's office and an extension specialist review the products before you use them.

## What it adds

- **Documents** the team issues: quarantine, hold order or control area notice; movement permit request and decision; presumptive positive and confirmation; premises investigation form; depopulation and disposal plan; indemnity claim worksheet; producer letter; fair or show cancellation notice; consumer questions and answers; worker health monitoring log.
- **Charts and maps** drawn from data: a premises map with zone rings, a premises table, a movement control map, a depopulation and disposal tracker, a sampling schedule by designation, and premises over flood zones. The curve of confirmed premises and the tracing diagram come from the epidemiology toolkit.
- **Decisions** for players to record: order a quarantine, establish a control area, change a designation, decide a movement permit, set the depopulation order, choose a disposal site, request federal resources or an emergency declaration, release a control area and publish a notice.
- **Writing guidance and checks** so drafts use the plan words and numbers, and go back for repair when a zone is under the plan minimum, a premises becomes monitored without being at-risk, a depopulation plan runs past 24 hours with no reason, or a confirmation comes from a network laboratory.

## Reading the response plans' designations and zones

The plans give every premises one designation. The app uses the plan words and shows each with a shape and an abbreviation as well as a color.

| Designation | Mark on the map | Meaning                                                                    |
| ----------- | --------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Infected    | star, IP        | Confirmed or presumptive positive premises                                 |
| Contact     | square, CP      | Linked to an infected premises by tracing                                  |
| Suspect     | diamond, SP     | Under investigation                                                        |
| At-risk     | triangle, ARP   | In or near the zones with a reason to watch it                             |
| Monitored   | open circle, MP | An at-risk premises with negative tests, allowed to move product by permit |
| Free        | small circle    | No reason to suspect disease                                               |
| Vaccinated  | cross, VP       | Secondary designation for premises with vaccinated animals                 |

Only an at-risk premises can become a monitored premises. The zones are the infected zone around an infected premises, the buffer zone beyond it (together the control area), and the surveillance zone outside the control area. For avian influenza the plan (the HPAI Red Book, May 2017) sets minimums of 3 km for the infected zone, 7 km more for the buffer zone, a control area at least 10 km from the closest infected premises and a surveillance zone at least 10 km wide. The foot-and-mouth plan (October 2020) gives sampling intervals by designation: contact premises every 5 days for 14 days, at-risk and monitored premises every 10 days and surveillance zone premises every 21 days, until 56 days after the last detected case. Each product names the plan and edition it follows, because a newer edition can change a number.

A **presumptive positive** is reported by a network laboratory. The national reference laboratory confirms it. The plan clocks, including the goal of depopulating within 24 hours, start at the presumptive result.

## Using the zone calculator

Give the calculator the infected premises and the plan. It returns the zones and areas with their distances, the premises in each zone and the designations each could take, and any overlap: infected premises whose control areas overlap form one area.

- Avian influenza uses the plan minimums unless you change them. A change is recorded with your reason, and a size under the plan minimum is flagged.
- Foot-and-mouth disease has no built-in sizes. State all four from your plan.
- Distances run from the closest infected premises, and a premises exactly on a boundary counts as inside.

Draw the result with the premises map. Rings are listed in kilometers in the figure's text summary, so the map reads without the picture.

## Designing a continuity of business exercise

Zones stop movement; the exercise is usually about what keeps moving. Build it in four steps.

1. Generate simulated premises and spread for a fictional county (see below), and set a service target for permits, for example a decision within one day.
2. Put permit requests from unaffected premises in the first two days, some for product that is ready to ship and some from premises in the buffer zone.
3. Give the permit desk the movement permit decision form and the premises table. Players issue, condition or deny, with testing.
4. In the Evaluation tab read three numbers together: the share of permits decided within your target, the share of unaffected premises able to move product, and the depopulation timing for the infected premises. Denying a permit can be correct, so the share able to move is context and not a score.

## The limits of simulated spread

The app can generate fictional premises for a county from a farm inventory you set, and spread an infection between them by distance and by movement links such as a feed truck or a crew. A seed repeats a run exactly. Controls from a day you choose cut the movement links.

This is a teaching model. It is not fitted to any disease, farm or county, it carries no real premises, and it must never be read as a forecast of a real outbreak. Premises are placed points with made-up names, and every figure and dataset says simulated. The depopulation and vaccination models are queues with the crews, methods, disposal sites and cold chain limits you set; they show where a backlog forms, not what a real crew can do.

## Replaying real detections

The app can fetch the monthly count of confirmed avian influenza detections in flocks from the USDA APHIS dashboard to set the pace of a scenario in real history. As with other case data you choose the period, see what would be fetched and how large it is, and only after you agree does the app contact the APHIS dashboard site. No account or key is needed.

The export has the month and flock type only: no county, flock size or farm, and none is invented. The premises in your scenario stay fictional, and the real counts set only the pace and scale. Farm inventories by county are not fetched; enter the inventory for your fictional county yourself.

## Reference documents

A catalog-only reference library holds the HPAI and FMD response plans (the Red Books) and four FAD PReP manuals from USDA APHIS, each pinned by hash with its edition date. Two manuals are marked draft on their covers and the FMD plan carries a draft footer. Check the APHIS preparedness page for newer editions before relying on them.
