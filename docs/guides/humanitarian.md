# Humanitarian and international response

The humanitarian toolkit gives an exercise the work of aid organizations in a crisis abroad: situation reports, a shared picture of who is doing what where, coordination gaps, staff security decisions, supply pipelines and camp health. It suits international and national NGOs, UN coordination offices and cluster partners, response site teams, headquarters crisis management teams, humanitarian studies classes, and voluntary organizations active in disasters at home that want the same products in a community exercise.

Turn it on in the scenario's **Toolkits** tab (see [Toolkits](toolkits.md)). The app proposes it when the brief mentions a cluster, a camp, a refugee influx, a flash update, a 3W table, a kidnapping or detention, or a pipeline break. The **Humanitarian and international response** kind of organization has four role sets: a country office, a coordination office, a response site and a headquarters crisis management team. Every organization, person and crisis it produces is fictional.

## What it adds

- **Coordination documents:** flash update, 3W table, cluster meeting minutes, gap list, needs overview and response plan summary, funding status, and the Humanitarian Country Team record.
- **Field security documents:** incident report, risk assessment summary, movement request and approval, inter-agency security alert, crisis management team log and family update. They stay at the level of decisions and communications.
- **Supply and needs documents:** pipeline dashboard, shipment status, customs hold notice, procurement request, warehouse report, access note, rapid assessment, distribution report, camp health report, community feedback log and misinformation tracker.
- **Charts** drawn from data, never as pictures from an image model: a 3W matrix and map with the areas nobody reported in marked, needs against coverage, the pipeline projection with its break day, funding status, a staff incident timeline and an access constraints map. Each has a text summary and a data table.
- **Decisions** for players to record, such as requesting cluster activation, assigning a gap to a partner, stopping movements, hibernating or relocating staff, notifying a family, sharing a security alert, rerouting a shipment and declaring an outbreak response.
- **Measures:** how long each coordination gap took to assign and to cover and how many were still open at the end; people reached against the plan's targets; staff safety decisions against when a threat began; pipeline decisions against the break; and whether the country office, headquarters, the Humanitarian Country Team and donors say the same thing.

## Things the toolkit gets right

- Under the IASC cluster approach, a cluster is requested by the Humanitarian Coordinator after consulting the Humanitarian Country Team and activated by the Emergency Relief Coordinator with the IASC Principals' endorsement. The 2025 Humanitarian Reset gives the Humanitarian Coordinator and the country team more say over which clusters to activate or merge, and is consolidating the 11 global clusters into 8 (shelter with camp coordination, logistics with emergency telecommunications, and one protection cluster). Say in the scenario which arrangement applies; the app flags a draft where the Humanitarian Coordinator activates clusters alone without saying so.
- A refugee setting uses the refugee coordination model instead of clusters. Where refugees and internally displaced people are in the same area, clusters can run beside the refugee response; name the mixed setting in the scenario.
- Taking part in the inter-agency security framework does not make the UN responsible for an NGO's security.
- Days of stock are stock on hand divided by daily consumption, and the figure, the text and the measures all use the same arithmetic.
- A blank in a 3W table is a gap or a reporting failure, not proof that nobody is working there.
- A flash update is the short, numbered product of the first days of an emergency or a sharp escalation. The situation report follows at a set interval once the response is running.
- Drafts follow do no harm: they weigh how a distribution site, a list or a message could put people at risk, and keep beneficiary and protection data need-to-know. Staff security decisions say what they mean for national staff as well as international staff.
- Staff security and kidnapping scenarios never include negotiation tactics, ransom amounts or mechanics, or techniques for evading or resisting detention. Add a content notice and a wellbeing check for facilitators when a scenario involves harm to staff.

## Data you can bring in

Five sources can seed a scenario with real structure and baselines. None needs a key.

- The Humanitarian Data Exchange works without setup: the app identifies itself with its own name and help@actionstations.ai. You can enter your own application name and contact in **Settings** instead.
- ReliefWeb only answers application names it has approved. It stays off until you enter an approved name in **Settings**. Everything else works without it, including the disaster timeline from GDACS.

- **Operational presence (3W) by country** from the Humanitarian Data Exchange: which organizations report work in which sector and admin area.
- **Key figures by country** from the Humanitarian Data Exchange, for the year your scenario starts in: people in need, targeted and reached by sector from the response plan, refugees and asylum seekers the country hosts (with their main countries of origin), the latest count of internally displaced people, and the appeal's requirements and funding. A series of the same figures over ten years comes with them. These are the figures OCHA's key figures pages show, from the same sources.
- **Disaster events** for a country and period from GDACS, the Global Disaster Alert and Coordination System run by the European Commission and UN OCHA: earthquakes, tropical cyclones, floods, volcanic eruptions, droughts and forest fires, each with its alert level, severity, affected countries, GLIDE number and a link to the GDACS report. An event that lasts appears at its onset and at its last update. By default you get orange and red alerts, the events at a humanitarian scale; green alerts can be added for the smaller ones (there can be hundreds a week, so keep the period short). Give the country as a three-letter ISO code. Alert levels are GDACS's automatic estimates from the first hours of an event, not damage assessments.
- **ReliefWeb report titles** for a country and period, if you have an approved application name. They add the situation reports and flash updates that agencies published to the GDACS timeline. Only titles, dates, sources and links are kept.
- **Aid worker security incident counts** from the Aid Worker Security Database, for one country (two-letter code) or worldwide, for the ten years up to your scenario: major incidents per year, aid workers killed, wounded, kidnapped and detained, national and international staff, and the most common means and context of attack. Only these counts are kept; incident details, places and names are never stored. The latest incidents are provisional, and the summary says how many are not yet verified.

For the Humanitarian Data Exchange sources, the licence of each source dataset is read when you fetch and kept with the data; data under a licence that does not allow redistribution stays in your scenario and is left out of exported packs. You see an estimate of the size before you agree, and the app sends nothing about your plans to any of these services.

## Simulated data

The app can generate, for any seed and settings, arrivals and registration at a site, the impact of a sudden-onset disaster by admin area, partners reporting into a 3W table over time, an outbreak in a camp, a supply pipeline with lead times and a break, and a rising threat level on a road. Every set is labeled **simulated**, repeats exactly with the same seed, and writes the events the measures score decisions against. They are teaching models, not forecasts.

## Reference documents

Most humanitarian doctrine is not public domain, so the toolkit links rather than bundles. Look for the current edition of these on their publishers' sites:

- IASC publications on cluster coordination, the Transformative Agenda and emergency scale-up, at interagencystandingcommittee.org.
- The Saving Lives Together framework and the security guides of the Global Interagency Security Forum, at gisf.ngo.
- The Core Humanitarian Standard, at corehumanitarianstandard.org.
- The Sphere Handbook, at spherestandards.org. Sphere indicators are referenced by name and never copied, because the handbook's licence terms are unclear.
- INSARAG guidance for urban search and rescue exercises, at insarag.org.
- The UNHCR Emergency Handbook, at emergency.unhcr.org.

## Before you use it for real

The rules the toolkit follows were checked against IASC and UNHCR pages and the IASC cluster simplification proposal on 11 November 2026. The cluster system is changing country by country, so confirm the current structure where your scenario is set. Have a humanitarian operations lead who has run cluster or NGO coordination review a scenario, and a security manager review any staff security scenario, before you use it with participants. Every product is marked as an exercise product.
