# Prairie Spill Response Cooperative: River Response Strategies and Equipment

Prairie Spill Response Cooperative (the OSRO under contract with Ironbark Pipeline Company) · Duty Manager 24 hours: 316-555-0155 · Revision 6

## 1. The cooperative

Prairie Spill Response Cooperative is a member-owned oil spill removal organization with depots at Topeka, Kansas and Wichita, Kansas and a warehouse at Kansas City, Missouri. It has 38 trained responders on call and contracted subcontractors with boats, vacuum trucks and heavy equipment. The Duty Manager on the day of the exercise is Alicia Brandt. The cooperative commits Tier 1 equipment to the Prairie Bend segment within 6 hours of notification and Tier 2 within 24 hours.

## 3. Response times to the crossing

From Topeka to the Mill Ridge staging area: 2 hours by truck. From Wichita: 3.5 hours. First boom is deployed at the nearest boat ramp 3.5 hours after notification. Skimmers are on the river after 5 hours.

## 4. River boom strategies

Kansas River conditions in June: flow around 8,500 cubic feet per second, a surface velocity of about 2.3 miles per hour (2 knots), a wide braided channel with sandbars, banks of sand and cottonwoods. Boom placed square to the current loses oil under it above about 0.7 knot. In a current of 2 knots the cooperative angles boom to deflect oil to a recovery pocket on the bank. Boom is anchored with stakes or sandbags and checked every 2 hours.

- **Containment boom:** a 600-foot U-shaped boom with skimmer at the apex, at a slow eddy.
- **Deflection boom:** a series of 400-foot sections at about 15 degrees to the current, directing oil toward a recovery pocket.
- **Exclusion boom:** protects an intake structure or a backwater (Cottonwood Slough).
- **Sorbent boom** is used at slow backwaters for sheen only.

Boom plans give site, length, type, anchoring, who deploys and when. Tactics for the intake at mile 11 are: deflection boom at mile 9.5 and exclusion boom at the intake. For the Sterling Bluff intake at mile 34: three deflection stations at miles 28, 31 and 33 and exclusion boom at the intake.

## 5. Trajectory and timing

A trajectory gives a window of arrival time for each site and the date and time of the model run. Oil on a river travels near the surface velocity: at 2.3 mph the mile 11 intake is reached after about 4.8 hours and the mile 34 intake about 14.8 hours after oil enters the river. Winds can slow or speed this by 10 to 20 percent. The Environmental Unit reruns the trajectory every 6 hours or when river flow changes by more than 15 percent.

## 6. Wildlife

Nesting piping plovers use sandbars at miles 6 to 9 in June. The cooperative's wildlife team works only under the direction of the US Fish and Wildlife Service and the state wildlife agency; hazing and capture are permitted only by authorized personnel. The wildlife status report counts oiled live, oiled dead, hazed, in rehabilitation and released.

## 7. Shoreline cleanup

Shoreline Cleanup Assessment Technique (SCAT) teams survey each segment and report the oiling category and the treatment recommended. On sand, manual removal and flushing are preferred; heavy equipment is not used on sandbars with nests.

## 8. Safety

A site safety plan is prepared for each work area: air monitoring for benzene and hydrogen sulfide near the source (medium sour crude can release H2S), personal flotation devices for all on the water, a spill safety zone with a radius of 200 yards around the source, and heat stress controls in June.
